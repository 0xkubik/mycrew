# copywriter-conversational — write a popular text the way this author tells one

Standing rules for conversational texts, drawn from the author's Habr article and its teaser, and
from where the formal-prose guidance stops. Nothing here is a step: read it before the first line,
check the draft against it after. For journals and conference papers use copywriter-scientific.

## Voice

- **First person.** "I tried, it failed, here is why"; "we" only for a whole community.
- **Frame claims as experience,** not law: "for me", "in my view", "honestly", "I concluded".
- **Plain spoken words are welcome:** "dumped everything on it", "patched the hole in a hurry".
- **A rhetorical question now and then,** always answered in the next breath.
- **No exclamation marks.** Humour comes from the comparison, never from punctuation.
- **Explain by analogy:** a baby superhero, a recipe that changes each step, one line each.
- **Reuse the picture.** A cone or a compass returns until it answers the opening question.
- **Long, dash-turned sentences beside short verdicts:** "Getting used to it."
- **Sentence length swings:** about one in five over 30 words, one in eight under 8.
- **Use the audience's own jargon** — roadmap, PoC, vibe coding — and explain a term once.

## Story and structure

- **Title is a claim or an order,** with a one-line thesis quote beneath it.
- **Open on shared ground:** a piece the audience already read, linked, then "I read it and tried".
- **Hook on one word** from the source that you cannot let go of: "sufficient — how much?"
- **Tell the failure before the fix:** what I tried, how it broke, what I understood.
- **Turning point through a quote:** a friend's remark or the source, set apart in a blockquote.
- **Answer the opening question late,** as a test the reader can run on themselves.
- **Scale the answer by the cost of a mistake:** a one-evening PoC, a real build, a scalable one.
- **One list only, for parallel habits:** a bold first-person verb, then the why; unequal length.
- **Walk through one example step by step,** growing a code block, ending on the punchline.
- **Name your own product plainly:** what it is, how you define its terms, commands in code font.
- **Two or three headings that sound like speech,** unnumbered: "Let's figure it out."
- **Close on the human side,** not a recap: a personal question answered both ways, then one word.
- **The teaser is its own text:** the stumble, the finding, one "Inside:" line, not the opening.

## Ground and honesty

- **Every idea comes from the author's theses.** Expand them; propose extras apart, never insert.
- **Quote sources exactly,** with title and link; never bend a quote into your own voice.
- **Explain an unfamiliar concept plainly,** with a concrete example, before you use it.
- **Say the real name** — the project, the tool, "since the Copilot beta" — never a stand-in.

## The machine tells that still apply

- **Vary the sentence shape.** Not every sentence "claim — elaboration"; use "since", "if…then".
- **No "first, next, finally" lockstep.** Unequal weight for points, no numbered lists in a story.
- **Three items of one length is a tell.** Cut one, or make the weakest visibly shorter.
- **Echo your own wording** for a repeated idea; a fresh synonym each time reads as a model.
- **Lead with the claim;** the confession or complication comes after it.
- **No filler openers:** "it is worth noting", "it is important to stress", "this shows that…".
